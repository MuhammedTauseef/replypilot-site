A Valentine’s Morning, Hour by Hour

Valentine’s Day, 8:00am. Your shop opens to a phone already buzzing — forty unread messages, and the van leaves at ten. Without automation, you spend the first hour typing instead of arranging, and every minute of typing is a bouquet that does not get made. With automation, the morning runs like this.

8:05: the first wave of orders is captured — occasion, bouquet choice, address, card message, payment link sent. 8:40: payment confirmations roll in and the orders queue up in the order they must be arranged. 9:30: the van is loaded with the first route, addresses already confirmed back to each customer. Midday: your same-day cutoff hits, and the chatbot starts telling new messages, politely, that their flowers will arrive tomorrow — no promises you cannot keep.

Evening: the “where is my order?” messages arrive, and the chatbot answers each one with the real delivery window — because you updated it once, and it told everyone. You spent the day doing what only you can do: arranging flowers. The chatbot did what it does best: capturing every order the second it arrived.

What Happens When the Exact Flowers Are Gone?

Mid-morning on a busy day, the peonies run out. Three customers have already asked for them. This is the moment that decides whether automation helps or hurts — because a chatbot that keeps promising peonies is worse than no chatbot at all.

The rule is simple: the chatbot only offers what you actually have. When something sells out, you mark it unavailable once, and from that second the chatbot pivots: “The peonies have just sold out — but the garden roses in blush are beautiful today, and I can show you a photo. Would you like those instead?” It shows a real photo, states the real price, and waits for a yes.

Customers accept substitutions gracefully when they are asked. What they never forgive is opening the box to find flowers they did not choose. The chatbot’s honesty here is not just good service — it is the difference between a repeat customer and a lost one.

How to Handle a Funeral Order With Care

Some orders need more than speed. A funeral order arrives from someone grieving, often in a hurry, often unsure what to send. The chatbot must slow down here — gentle words, simple choices, zero sales pressure.

Teach it a separate tone for these orders: short sentences, no upselling, no cheerful exclamation marks. It asks only what it needs: the name for the card — spelled carefully, confirmed twice — the service time and venue, and whether the family has requested a particular style. It confirms the delivery time against the service, not against your convenience.

And it knows when to step aside. Anything unusual — a request in another language for the card, a delivery to a home instead of the service, a customer who seems distressed in their messages — goes straight to you, with the full conversation attached. Some moments need a human voice. The chatbot’s job is to recognise those moments and get out of the way.

What Should the Chatbot Do About a Late or Wrong Delivery?

Even good shops have bad days. The van gets stuck in traffic. The wrong bouquet goes out. What happens in the next ten minutes matters more than the mistake itself — and the chatbot is usually the first to hear about it.

“Where are my flowers?” The chatbot never guesses. It checks the real delivery status and gives an honest answer: “Your bouquet is on the van — the driver is running about thirty minutes behind because of traffic. The new arrival window is 4:30 to 5:00.” If it does not know, it says so: “Let me check with the shop right now and come straight back to you.” Then it tells you immediately.

Here is what owners learn: a late delivery handled honestly often keeps the customer. A late delivery met with silence or excuses loses them. The chatbot makes sure every complaint gets an instant, honest first reply — and makes sure you hear about it while there is still time to fix it.

How to Teach the Chatbot Your Delivery Zones

Every flower shop has a map in its head: the streets you serve daily, the far suburb you only reach on Fridays, the village you do not serve at all. Customers do not know this map — so they ask, one by one, “do you deliver to my area?” Teach the chatbot the map once, and it answers forever.

Write down your zones in plain words: which areas get same-day delivery, which get next-day, what the delivery charge is for each, and where you simply do not go. The chatbot checks every address against this list before confirming an order. “We deliver to your area on Tuesdays and Fridays — shall I book yours for Friday?” is a far better answer than a vague promise.

The hidden win: fewer failed deliveries. Most failed flower deliveries are not wrong addresses — they are addresses outside the real delivery area that someone accepted anyway. A chatbot that knows your map says no early, kindly, before flowers are arranged and fuel is burned.

Should the Chatbot Sell Flower Subscriptions?

One-off orders are wonderful, but they make every month a fresh hunt. Subscriptions — a fresh bouquet for the office every Monday, or for the home on the first of the month — turn one sale into twelve. And the chatbot is a natural subscription seller, because it never forgets to ask.

The moment fits naturally after a happy delivery. A few days later: “Glad the birthday bouquet went down well! Many of our customers get fresh flowers every two weeks — shall I set that up for you?” No pressure, just an offer at the exact moment the customer is happiest with your flowers. Offices, salons, restaurants and receptions are the best targets — places where fresh flowers are part of the look.

Subscriptions also smooth out your quiet weeks. Valentine’s and Mother’s Day will always spike, but a base of regular deliveries keeps the van moving — and the cash coming — in between. It is the steadiest income a flower shop can build, and the chatbot sells it while you arrange.
